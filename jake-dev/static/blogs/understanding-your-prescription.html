Understanding Your Glasses Prescription: A Complete Guide

Those numbers and abbreviations on your prescription don't have to be confusing. Here's what every value means and why it matters for your glasses.

Sample Prescription SPH (Sphere) CYL & AXIS ADD PD FAQs

What Does a Prescription Look Like?

Every glasses prescription contains the same basic information, though the layout varies between opticians. Here's a typical example:

Sample Prescription

Issued: 26 November 2025

SPH CYL AXIS ADD
Right (OD) -2.50 -0.75 180 +2.00
Left (OS) -3.00 -1.00 175 +2.00

PD: 63mm (or R: 31.5 / L: 31.5)

OD and OS Explained

OD (Oculus Dexter) = Right eye. OS (Oculus Sinister) = Left eye. Some prescriptions simply say "R" and "L" instead.

SPH (Sphere) - Your Main Prescription Power

The SPH value is the main measurement that corrects your overall vision. It tells us how much lens power you need and whether you're short-sighted or long-sighted.

Minus (-) Numbers

Short-sighted (Myopia)

  • Difficulty seeing distant objects clearly
  • Near vision is usually fine
  • Example: -2.50 means moderately short-sighted

Plus (+) Numbers

Long-sighted (Hyperopia)

  • Close-up objects appear blurry
  • Distance vision may be clearer
  • Example: +1.50 means mildly long-sighted

How Strong is My Prescription?

Mild
+/-2.25 to +/-5.00
Moderate

Lens Tip: Higher prescriptions benefit from thinner lens options. We recommend 1.67 or 1.74 index lenses for prescriptions over +/-4.00 to keep your glasses lightweight and attractive.

CYL (Cylinder) & AXIS - Astigmatism Correction

These two values work together to correct astigmatism - a common condition where your eye isn't perfectly spherical. If these boxes are empty or say "DS" (Dioptre Sphere), you don't have astigmatism.

CYL (Cylinder)

The amount of astigmatism correction needed.

  • Usually written as a minus number
  • Typical range: -0.25 to -4.00
  • Higher numbers = more astigmatism

AXIS

The angle at which the cylinder correction is placed.

  • A number between 1 and 180
  • Represents degrees of rotation
  • Only present if you have CYL

What is Astigmatism?

Think of a perfect eye as shaped like a football (soccer ball) - perfectly round. An eye with astigmatism is shaped more like a rugby ball - curved more in one direction than the other. This causes light to focus at multiple points instead of one, making vision blurry at all distances.

Good news: Astigmatism is extremely common and easily corrected with glasses. About 1 in 3 people have some degree of astigmatism.

Important: CYL and AXIS must always be entered together. The AXIS tells the lab exactly how to position the cylinder correction in your lens - even a few degrees off can affect your vision.

ADD (Addition) - Reading & Near Vision Power

The ADD value is extra magnifying power for reading and close work. It's typically prescribed for people over 40 who have presbyopia - the natural loss of near focusing ability that comes with age.

Key Facts About ADD

  • Always a plus (+) number, typically +0.75 to +3.50
  • Usually the same for both eyes
  • Added to your distance prescription for reading glasses
  • Used in varifocals, bifocals, and reading glasses

How ADD is Used in Different Lens Types

Varifocals (Progressive Lenses)

The ADD power gradually increases from the top of the lens (distance) to the bottom (reading), with intermediate zones in between.

Bifocals

The ADD power is in the visible reading segment at the bottom of the lens. No intermediate zone.

Reading Glasses

Your distance SPH + your ADD = your reading prescription. For example: -2.50 SPH + 2.00 ADD = -0.50 reading power.

Age Guide: Most people start needing an ADD around age 40-45. It typically increases gradually until around age 60, when it usually stabilises at around +2.50 to +3.00.

PD (Pupillary Distance) - Essential for Online Orders

Your PD is the distance in millimetres between the centres of your pupils. It's crucial for positioning the optical centre of each lens directly in front of your eyes. Without accurate PD, even a perfect prescription won't work properly.

Single PD

One measurement for both eyes combined.

Example: PD: 63mm

Adult range: typically 54-74mm

Dual PD

Separate measurements for each eye (more accurate).

Example: R: 31.5 / L: 31.5

Most faces are slightly asymmetrical

PD Often Not on Your Prescription

UK opticians aren't legally required to include PD on your prescription. If it's missing, you can: ask your optician to measure it (they may charge a small fee), or use our PD measurement guide when ordering.

Why PD Matters

If your PD is wrong by just 2-3mm, you may experience:

  • Eye strain and headaches
  • Blurry or distorted vision
  • Double vision in severe cases
  • Difficulty adapting to new glasses

Other Terms You Might See

PRISM

Corrects eye alignment issues. Measured in prism dioptres with a direction (Base In, Out, Up, or Down). Not common.

BVD (Back Vertex Distance)

The distance from your eye to the lens. Important for high prescriptions. Standard is 12mm.

DS (Dioptre Sphere)

Written in the CYL column when there's no astigmatism. Means "sphere only".

Plano (PL)

Means zero power. That eye doesn't need correction for that particular measurement.

NV or Near

Near vision prescription. Sometimes written separately from distance (DV).

Int or Intermediate

Intermediate distance prescription, typically for computer use. Usually half your ADD value.
